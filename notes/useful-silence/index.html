FIELD NOTE 06 / Communication

When silence creates space for better judgment

A little space can help you hear what was actually said—and notice what you were about to assume.

Yohannes Belai1 min read

Silence can make a conversation uncomfortable, especially when you feel responsible for keeping it moving. The temptation is to answer quickly, offer advice, or add your own story. Sometimes that is useful. Sometimes it closes a thought the other person was still trying to finish.

Leaving a little room allows you to listen beyond the first sentence. You may notice uncertainty, a distinction, or a concern that changes your understanding. A patient question can do more than a polished response delivered before you understand the issue.

Silence is not automatically wise. Withholding information can create confusion, and refusing to address a problem can damage trust. The useful distinction is whether the quiet supports understanding or merely helps you avoid discomfort.

Speak when clarity, responsibility, or care calls for it. When you need time, say so and return to the conversation. Used that way, a pause is neither a performance of mystery nor a tactic for controlling someone. It is room to think and room for another person to be heard.

A QUESTION TO SIT WITH

Am I about to add clarity, or just fill the quiet?
